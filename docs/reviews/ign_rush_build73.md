# Rush Review: The Tickets Are the Enemy

**Platform:** Web / Android (played on a phone-sized browser) - **Version reviewed:** v0.1.73

Most cooking games want you to tap fast. Rush wants you to think. It takes the panic of a dinner service, freezes it into turns, and hands you a line cook or two to move around a five-station kitchen like squad members on a tactical map. Tickets print, the clock moves a minute every time you end your turn, and a table that waits too long walks out. It is a clever premise, and it is carried out more neatly than I expected from a small phone game. After about half an hour I had cleared three full services and was deep into a fourth, with a shop full of upgrades and a kitchen that had grown from two stations to five. I wanted to keep going, which is the best thing I can say about it.

## Gameplay

Each turn your cooks get three moves, shown as little dots over their heads. Tap a station to select whoever is standing there, then use the one big context button at the bottom of the screen: FIRE a dish, PLATE it when it is done, RUN plates from the pass to the dining room, or RUSH a pan to shave a minute off it at the cost of stress. END TURN advances the clock, and every ticket's timer bar drains. That single smart button is the heart of the design. It always tells you what it will do ("DEV: FIRE T10", "MO: PLATE", "DEV: RUN 2 PLATES"), and END TURN warns you when you are about to make a mistake ("END TURN / GRILL BURNS"). I ignored that warning once in service three, lost a steak, and had to refire it with the table's patience down to a single minute. That near miss was the most exciting moment of my session, and it was entirely my fault.

The planning is the fun part. A ticket for a BIG table might need pasta, wings, steak and scampi from four different stations. You learn to fire long cooks first, to park one cook at the pass as a runner while the other works the line, and to time a plating so the whole table's food lands on the pass together and goes out in one run. The NEXT bar ("2 dishes in 1 min, BIG table in 3 min") lets you see trouble coming, and a "+N MORE" badge pages through the ticket rail when it overflows. When it clicks, a turn feels like a little puzzle you solved rather than a frantic scramble.

Services escalate well. Service one is just grill and fry. Sauté opens in service two, along with a twist; mine was a food critic whose ticket scored extra if it went out clean. Service three adds a COLD station for salads and cakes, and service four opened with "Grill on the Fritz", which made every grill dish a minute slower. Each twist is announced on a clear card before the service starts, so you can plan around it.

Between services, a grade sheet scores you out of 100 (tickets out, speed, clean pans, critic bonus) and pays tips into a shop. Upgrades last the whole night: a Server Tray that carries two plates per run, Heat Lamps for an extra minute on every ticket, Family Meal for one more point of stress before a cook quits, Cast Iron to make rushing free, Comp Cards to forgive a walked table, and a pricier Entree Tray. The choices are real. Heat Lamps made service three noticeably calmer, and Cast Iron turned RUSH from a reluctant emergency tool into something I used every turn. The game also adjusts quietly: after an A it told me "the house noticed: one more ticket", which is a neat, honest way to tune difficulty without hiding it.

Where it stumbles is movement. Walking between stations costs moves based on distance, and that matters a great deal, but the game never shows it. More than once I tapped a far station with a cook selected and nothing happened at all, because the cook did not have enough moves left to get there. There is no message and no highlight, just a tap that does nothing. Another time a cook walked two stations and quietly spent every move she had left. Once you work it out you can plan around it, but a step count on the target station, or even a "TOO FAR" line, would save every new player the same confusion. Selecting a cook is also a little fiddly. Tapping a station picks whoever is standing at it, and a second tap sends your previous pick there. That is powerful when you understand it, but it is easy to end up with the wrong cook selected.

## Presentation

The kitchen looks good: a painted backdrop of copper pots and white tile, chunky station cards with clear food icons, and cook portraits with their own stress meters underneath. Each ticket card lists its dishes in plain words ("at GRILL", "cooking", "on the pass", struck-through "served"), and the timer bars turn yellow, then red, as a table gets impatient. The pass shows little plates stacking up, so you can read the state of the whole kitchen at a glance, and it all fits on a narrow phone screen without feeling crowded.

The audio is a pleasant surprise. Real kitchen recordings (a sizzle when you fire, a printer chattering out tickets, a bell on the pass) sit under a jazzy loop, and every important sound gets a soft caption such as "[sizzle: rushed]" or "[printer: new ticket]". Pause works as it should: QUIT to the title and CONTINUE picked up the same minute of the same service, with the same orders on the rail. Settings are compact and sensible, with a pace option, volume sliders and a best-night record.

The intro cards and HOW TO PLAY screen are short and clear. A handful of coach hints in the first service teach FIRE, PLATE, RUN and END TURN, then get out of the way.

## Difficulty

On normal pace, the first three services felt fair and well balanced. Service one is a gentle tutorial, two asks you to start juggling, and three, with four stations and a BIG table on the rail, needed real planning. I came through all three without a single walked table, and the third earned an A. Service four, with the grill slowed down and a 12-top on the rail, was the first time the rail stayed full of red-flagged tickets and both cooks ran out of moves every turn. That is roughly the right curve. If anything, the first two services are a little too forgiving for anyone who has played a tactics game. The stress system, which ends your night if a cook quits, now has a visible meter and a TIRED warning, but in my run I never came close to losing a cook. The real pressure came from the clock.

## Pros

- A smart, original premise: kitchen management played as a turn-based tactics game
- One context button that always says what it will do, plus clear warnings before you make a mistake
- New stations and nightly twists (critic, grill on the fritz) keep each service fresh
- The shop makes meaningful choices, and its upgrades noticeably change how you play
- Readable, attractive kitchen art; ticket cards say exactly where every dish is
- Real kitchen sound with captions; pause and continue resume the exact same minute

## Cons

- Walking costs moves based on distance, but the game never shows it; a tap on a station that is too far does nothing, with no feedback
- Cook selection (tap a station to pick, tap again to send) is easy to get wrong
- The RUSH button still reads "+STRESS" after you buy Cast Iron, which makes rushing free
- The first two services are too easy for experienced players; stress rarely feels threatening
- Only two cooks, and no hiring or extra staff in the shop, so the late game turns into the same two-person shuffle

## Score: 7.5 / 10 - Good

## Verdict

Rush is a sharp idea, built with care. It turns a dinner rush into a calm, readable tactics puzzle where every move counts and the tension comes from the clock, not from your reflexes. A context button that always tells you what it will do, warnings before you burn something, upgrades that change how you play, and twists that rewrite each service give it real "one more night" pull. It falls short of great mainly because of how cooks move: walking costs are invisible, too-far taps fail silently, and selecting the right cook takes more care than it should. The early services also let experienced players coast. Show the step costs on the board, punish complacency a little sooner, and give the late game more to manage than two cooks, and Rush could become the rare phone strategy game you recommend to people who do not play strategy games.
