# Rush Review: XCOM, But the Aliens Are Table Seven's Wings

*Played: web build v0.1.70, portrait, in a mobile-sized browser window. Free.*

There is a moment in every restaurant shift when the ticket printer won't stop and the whole line has to decide, out loud, who is doing what. Rush turns that moment into a turn-based puzzle. You don't cook in real time. You get two cooks, three actions each per turn, and a rail of tickets whose clocks drop a minute every time you press END TURN. It's a smart pitch, and on a phone screen it mostly works. It's also a game that hides its most important rule until that rule ends your night.

## Gameplay

A service is a row of five stations (grill, fry, saute, cold, and the pass) with your cooks, Dev and Mo, standing beneath them. Tap a cook, tap a station, and they walk over. Once there, the big context button does the obvious thing: FIRE a dish, PLATE it when the pan says UP!, RUN plates from the pass to the floor. Pans cook over turns, so the whole game is timing. You want Dev to plate the burger on the same turn the fries come up, and Mo to be standing at the pass by the time both are waiting there. When it clicks, it really feels like running a good line.

The best ideas are small ones. The button names your next move ("FIRE T17", "RUN 2 PLATES"), and END TURN warns you about the next disaster ("END TURN / FRY BURNS", "END TURN / MO QUITS"). You can tap a ticket to make it the priority. RUSH spends stress to shave a minute off a pan. A NEXT line tells you what is coming ("BIG table in 2 min"). Each service adds something: saute opens in service 2, and service 3 brings a COLD station for salads and cakes plus a nightly twist. Mine were "Grill on the fritz" and a "Bus Tour" that drops three tables at once.

The problem is stress. Walking costs one action per station, so a cook at the fry station spends their whole turn just reaching the pass, and nothing on screen says so until the dots run out. Stress then climbs faster than I could follow. Both of my cooks went from 1/6 to TIRED 4/6 in about two turns, and the "IDLE COOKS LOSE STRESS" hint showed up exactly when I could no longer afford to let anyone idle. My first night ended in service 3 with one ticket left on the rail and the whole line walking out. The game did warn me with "END TURN / MO QUITS", but only once Mo had no moves left. The warning is good. It just comes a turn too late to act on.

## Presentation

Rush looks better than most free web games. The title screen is a confident burst of flaming order slips under a chunky orange RUSH logo. The kitchen is a painted backdrop of copper pans and a hood vent, and the dish icons (burger, wings, a cake with a cherry) read clearly at phone size. Tickets turn from green to amber to red, there are stress pips under each cook, and a big A gets stamped on the grade sheet. In general the UI does a lot with little space.

It does get crowded. Coach hints like "IDLE COOKS LOSE STRESS" sit right on top of the station labels. When the rail fills up, the ticket text gets tiny and you have to page with "+3 MORE". At one point two different tickets were both labelled T22. I couldn't hear the audio in my setup, so I can't judge the sound. The captions ("[sizzle: rushed]", "[door slams: cook walked out]", "[register: heat lamps bought]") suggest real kitchen sound design, and they're a nice accessibility touch.

## Onboarding

The intro cards, a HOW TO PLAY page that scrolls and tells you to keep scrolling, and short coach hints ("TAP FIRE TO COOK", "PASS: RUN SERVES IT") got me through service 1 with no mistakes. Service 1 is a gentle sandbox, and I got an A. The gap is in the systems that end runs: what walking costs, why stress goes up, and how to bring it down. I only learned them by losing. A one-line "walking costs a move per station" and a stress tooltip on the first TIRED would fix most of it.

## Replayability

Between services you take your tips to a Kitchen Shop. A Server Tray lets you carry two plates per run, Heat Lamps add a minute to every ticket, Cast Iron makes RUSH free, and Comp Cards let one extra table walk. Each service is graded on a 100-point score (out, speed, clean pans), with a note that "the house noticed" and is adding a ticket. There's a PACE setting (Relaxed / Normal / Slammed) and a best-night record on the title screen. The pieces of a good roguelite loop are all here. What's missing is a reason to come back: a night resets to service 1, I couldn't find anything that carries over between nights apart from the high score, and three or four twists won't stay fresh for long.

## Verdict

Rush is the rare phone strategy game with a premise you get instantly and a turn structure that suits a few minutes on a bus. Firing, plating and running on a shared clock really does make you feel like you're keeping a line alive, and the UI's habit of spelling out your next move and its consequences is excellent. It falls short of great because its central fail state, stress, is hard to read until it ends your run, because walking costs eat whole turns without telling you, and because a night has too little variety to keep you hooked after a couple of runs. It's a sharp, promising slice, not yet a full meal.

## Score: 7.0 / 10 (Good)

**Pros**
- Clever, easy-to-explain idea: turn-based tactics where the tickets are the enemy
- Context buttons that name the action and warn you ("END TURN / FRY BURNS") make it readable on a phone
- A new station and a nightly twist each service keep the difficulty rising
- Attractive painted kitchen and title art, and a satisfying grade stamp
- Shop upgrades give you real choices between services

**Cons**
- Stress jumps to quitting levels in two turns, and the warning only comes once you can't do anything about it
- What walking costs (one move per station) is never shown and eats whole turns
- Coach banners cover the station labels, and a full rail makes the ticket text small; I also saw two tickets both labelled T22
- Losing sends you back to service 1, and nothing seems to carry over between nights apart from your best score
- Not much content yet: four stations' worth of dishes and only a handful of twists
