# Rush Review: Two Cooks, Four Stations, One Very Long Night

**Build reviewed: v0.1.76 (web build, played on a phone-sized screen)**

Every restaurant kitchen has a moment, usually around 7:15, when the printer will not stop and the person on fry is staring at the person on saute as if one of them is about to cry. Rush is built entirely around that moment. It is a turn-based tactics game in which the enemy is the ticket rail, your squad is two line cooks named Dev and Mo, and every action point you spend walking to the pass is one you did not spend saving a steak. It is a sharp, honest idea, and for most of a night it plays beautifully. Then it hits a wall, and it is worth talking about where that wall is.

## Gameplay

Each turn, both cooks get three moves. Walking between stations costs moves, firing a dish costs one, plating costs one, and running finished plates from the pass to the dining room costs one. Pans cook in real minutes of the service clock, and each end of turn ticks the clock forward. Tickets slide onto a rail at the top of the screen with a countdown and a green, then yellow, then red bar; let one hit zero and the table walks out. Three walks and the night is over.

The genius is in how the pieces lock together. The pass window means nobody carries plates around, so the real puzzle becomes positioning: who stays on the grill, who drifts to cold, who is going to be standing at the pass on the turn that four plates land there at once. RUSH lets a cook knock a minute off a pan for a point of stress, and stress is the game's second clock. Cooks who work all turn get more stressed; cooks who stand still recover. Push someone to six and they walk out the back door. It is XCOM's overwatch-versus-move dilemma translated into "do I let Mo breathe this turn, or do I need those fries."

The interface does a lot of quiet work to make this readable. Tap a cook and every station shows "WALK 1," "WALK 2" or "TOO FAR." The action button spells out exactly what will happen: "MO: FIRE T3," "DEV: RUSH (FREE)," "DEV: NO MOVES." Ticket rows read "at GRILL," "cooking," "on the pass" and "served" with a strikethrough. A pan about to scorch says PLATE NOW, and the END TURN button itself changes to "END TURN / COLD BURNS" or "END TURN / DEV NEAR QUIT." When a warning matters, it is printed on the button you are about to press, which is exactly where it belongs.

The structure is a night of services. Service 1 is just grill and fry, saute opens in service 2, a cold station (salads and cake) arrives in service 3, and each service adds a ticket. Every service from the second on has a twist: a food critic whose ticket moves your grade, a VIP table worth triple tips that counts double if walked, a 12-top that floods the rail with four dishes. Between services, tips go into a shop: a bigger serving tray, heat lamps that add a minute to every ticket, cast iron that makes RUSH stress-free, a family meal that raises the quit threshold, comp cards that forgive a walk. The upgrades are genuinely transformative; the four-plate Entree Tray turned my service 4 runs into single-tap sweeps, and free rushes reshaped how I used every turn.

I cleared services 1 through 3 with top grades (87 and 94 points on the last two, with the critic served for a bonus), and the game felt terrific: dense, readable decisions, real combo turns where one cook plates, fires and rushes while the other walks to the pass and clears the window. Service 4 is where it broke me. Twelve tickets, four open stations, a VIP and a 12-top, and still only two cooks. By the middle of the service I had four dishes waiting on fry, three on saute, and a full pass with nobody close enough to run it. Both cooks crept to TIRED, a table walked, and then the whole line walked out in a single end of turn.

That last moment is the game's biggest stumble. The END TURN button warned "DEV QUITS." It did not warn that Mo was going too, and the game over screen read "THE WHOLE LINE WALKED OUT." Whether Mo was pushed over the edge by Dev leaving or simply was not called out on the button, I lost a night I thought I was about to limp through with one cook. For a game that has worked so hard to put every consequence on the button, this one felt unfair.

## Presentation

Rush looks better than most phone strategy games at this stage. The kitchen is a warm painted backdrop of brick, copper pans and hanging lamps, the stations have chunky illustrated icons, and the food art (burger, steak, scampi, cake) is appetizing and instantly legible. The two cooks are friendly cartoon portraits with a stress meter of little boxes underneath, and the meter previews what end of turn will do with red and green outlines and a "+1" or "-2."

What it lacks is life. Cooks glide between stations as portraits rather than people; nothing sizzles visually beyond a ring timer, and the busiest moment of the night looks almost the same as the first ticket. Sound does more lifting: stove clicks, a printer chattering out new tickets, a bell, a chair scrape when a table walks, a door slam when a cook quits, all over a cafe-jazz track that gets busier as the rail goes red. Captions for every cue are on by default, which is a lovely touch, though on a busy screen they compete with the status line and the event text for the same strip of space.

The menus are tidy. The title, a short three-card intro, a scrolling HOW TO PLAY, settings with a pace option, and a pause menu all behave. The grade card at the end of a service is a satisfying beat: a big letter, a breakdown of points for tickets out, speed and clean pans, and a note when the house decides you are good enough for an extra ticket next time.

## Difficulty

The curve is the thing holding Rush back. Services 1 to 3 are a smooth climb in which every new station feels like a new toy. Service 4 is a cliff. The game adds a fourth station and keeps the crew at two, so the core spatial puzzle shifts from "plan your positioning" to "accept that something will burn." A third cook, a hire in the shop, or a slower ramp on ticket count when cold opens would all keep the tension without making the night feel unwinnable.

The second issue is that losing resets the whole night. "A new night starts at service 1," the game over screen says, and it means it: you replay the gentle opening services and lose your upgrades. That is a fine roguelike loop if the early services are quick, but here they are 10 to 15 turns each, and a player who just lost at service 4 has 20 minutes of easy content between them and another go at the wall.

Finally, stress is still the hidden killer. The meter and the button warnings are a huge improvement, but the moment-to-moment cost of an action (does this plate push Dev from 4 to 5?) is not shown until end of turn. Experienced players learn to read the boxes; first-time players will mostly learn by losing a cook.

## Pros

- A genuinely original tactics premise that maps the kitchen rush onto action points cleanly
- Excellent readability: walk distances, ticket states and consequences printed on the button you press
- Services 1 to 3 build beautifully, with a new station and a twist each time
- Shop upgrades are meaningful and change how you play
- Warm painted kitchen and appetizing food art
- Strong, tactile sound with captions on by default

## Cons

- Service 4 is a difficulty cliff: four stations, a VIP and a 12-top with only two cooks
- A cook quitting can end the night with no warning for the second cook; the END TURN warning named only one
- Losing restarts the whole night from service 1, so retries are long and repetitive
- Stress cost of individual actions is not previewed until end of turn
- Cooks are static portraits; the kitchen never looks as frantic as it feels
- Bottom-of-screen text (captions, event lines, status) crowds together on busy turns; stale lines such as an earlier "TABLE 20 WALKED" linger

## Score: 7.5 / 10 - Good

## Verdict

Rush is the most interesting kitchen game I have played on a phone in a while, because it understands that a dinner rush is a positioning problem rather than a reflex test. For three services it is a tight, readable, deeply satisfying puzzle, with upgrades that matter and twists that make each service feel distinct. Then the night outgrows its two-cook crew, a warning you were trusting misses a second walkout, and the game sends you all the way back to 7:00 PM on service 1. Fix the curve, let the player keep a foothold after a loss, and give the kitchen some motion, and Rush could be special. As it stands, it is a very good game that stops being fair just when it gets exciting.
